# Coming back from a break

A practical summary of what the research says about returning to lifting after time off, and why
Ironterm's re-entry ramp and comeback programs work the way they do. Plain language first; the
sources are at the bottom, numbered like [1].

## The short version

- **Strength barely drops for the first 3–4 weeks off.** What you feel on day one back is mostly
  soreness and rustiness, not lost strength. Don't drop the weights much; drop the *volume*.
- **After about a month off, real strength loss starts** and grows with the length of the break.
  Older lifters lose it faster. It comes back faster than it took to build: roughly half the length
  of the break.
- **Squats hurt the most** because they load big muscles through a long range under a slow
  lowering. That's the textbook recipe for muscle damage and soreness, and the protection you
  build against it fades without regular exposure.
- **Squat often, but not much per session.** Small, frequent doses rebuild the protection against
  soreness quickly without causing much damage along the way. Once weekly volume is matched,
  frequency makes little difference to strength, so you lose nothing by spreading it out.
- **Cramping is mostly a nervous-system fatigue problem**, not a salt or water problem. The best
  evidence-backed fixes are being less fatigued (lighter, shorter sessions while you readapt) and
  stretching the cramping muscle. Hydration and electrolytes are fine but probably not the cause.

## How much strength do you actually lose?

| Time off | Typical strength change | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Up to ~2 weeks | Essentially none | Some stiffness and extra soreness, back to normal in 1–2 sessions |
| 2–4 weeks | Trivial (statistically near zero in young adults) | Noticeable soreness, especially legs; a few sessions to feel normal |
| 1–2 months | Small but real losses start | Start around 85% of your pre-break best and build back over 2–3 weeks |
| 2–6 months | Larger losses; muscle size drops back toward where it started | Start around 75–80% and expect several weeks to get back |
| 6+ months | Most of the size gains can be gone, but strength stays above where you first started | Treat it as a fresh start with a head start |

What the studies show:

- A meta-analysis of 103 studies found maximal strength is essentially unchanged for up to about
  four weeks off, with losses speeding up after that. Upper and lower body lost strength at
  similar rates; people over 65 lost about twice as much [1][2].
- In a bench press study, one group trained continuously for 24 weeks and another took a 3-week
  break after every 6 weeks of training. During the breaks, muscle size dropped but **strength was
  maintained**, and the group with breaks ended up with the same gains as the continuous group.
  They even gained faster right after each break [3]. A larger follow-up in untrained adults found
  the same final result: what was lost during the breaks came back quickly [4].
- When strength does drop, the rule of thumb from the literature is that **regaining it takes about
  half as long as the break** (a third to two thirds) [2]. In older men, gains from 12 weeks of
  training were partly kept after 12 weeks off, and 1RM came back quickly on retraining [5].
- Part of that early strength is skill (the movement pattern and nervous system), and it's very
  persistent. In one study, muscle size fell all the way back to baseline over 20 weeks off, but
  about 60% of the strength gain was still there [6].

### Is "muscle memory" real?

Partly. In animals, the extra nuclei a muscle fibre gains while it grows seem to stick around after
it shrinks, which could make regrowth faster [7]. Human evidence is mixed: one study found a
previously trained leg did **not** regrow faster than an untrained one over 5 weeks [6], and a
review concluded it's still unclear whether the mechanism holds in people [8]. The practical
takeaway is the same either way: strength and skill come back quickly; size comes back with
training, not by magic.

## Why squats are the worst part of coming back

Soreness after lifting (DOMS) comes from **eccentric** work, the lowering phase, and it is worst when
the muscle is stretched at long lengths under load [9]. A deep squat lowers a large muscle mass
(quads, glutes, adductors) under heavy load into a deep stretch. Bench and overhead press have
shorter ranges and smaller muscles; deadlifts barely have an eccentric if you lower them quickly.
That's why the first squat session back can leave you hobbling for days while your bench feels fine.

The **repeated bout effect** is why it doesn't feel this way when you train regularly: one bout of
eccentric work protects you against damage from the next one [10]. How long that lasts depends on
how hard the first bout was:

- After one very damaging bout, some protection lasts months (lost somewhere between 9 and 12
  months) [11].
- After a light "priming" bout, protection is clear at 2 weeks and **gone by 3 weeks** [12].
- Even a session of very light eccentrics (about 10% of max effort) cuts soreness from a later hard
  session by roughly a third to a half across many muscle groups [13]. One light bout gives about
  20% of the protection that 16 bouts give, so **protection builds with each exposure** [14].

Regular training is a stream of moderate bouts, closer to the "priming" case than the "one brutal
bout" case. So after 2–3 weeks off, much of your everyday protection is gone, and the first normal
squat session back is effectively an unaccustomed eccentric bout.

What this means in practice:

1. **The first session back should be light enough not to wreck you.** It still builds a lot of
   protection for the next one [13].
2. **Expose the lift often.** Each exposure adds protection, so three short squat sessions in a week
   beat one big one.
3. **Ease into depth and slow lowering.** Long-length, slow eccentrics do the most damage [9]. Use
   controlled but not exaggerated tempo at first; add paused or slow-eccentric work later.
4. **Lower body ramps slower than upper body.** Strength comes back at similar rates [1], but the
   soreness cost of squatting is much higher, so legs need more exposures before full volume.

## Frequency vs volume when returning

With weekly volume matched, training a lift more often doesn't build meaningfully more strength
[15][16]; one study in trained men found 3 and 6 sessions a week gave the same gains [17]. That cuts
both ways when you're coming back:

- You don't need high frequency for strength, **but** it costs nothing.
- Splitting the same weekly sets into smaller, more frequent doses keeps each session's damage
  low and gets you the repeated-exposure benefit above.

So the comeback pattern is **more sessions, fewer sets each**: for example squat 3–4 times a week
for 2–3 sets instead of once or twice for 5+ sets.

To *keep* strength during a break, much less is needed than you think. In younger adults, one
session a week with one hard set per exercise kept size and strength for up to 32 weeks, **as long
as the weight stayed heavy**; intensity matters most [18]. Older adults need about twice that.

## Cramping

Honest answer: the cause is debated, and the evidence for the popular explanation is weak.

- The idea that cramps come from dehydration or low electrolytes rests mostly on anecdotes and
  small case series. Prospective studies don't support it [19].
- The leading explanation is **altered neuromuscular control**: with fatigue, the reflexes that
  excite a muscle (from muscle spindles) overpower the ones that inhibit it (from tendon
  receptors), and the muscle locks up [19][20][21]. That fits why cramps show up when you're
  deconditioned and pushing an unaccustomed effort, which is exactly the first week back.
- What helps, roughly in order of evidence:
  1. **Be less fatigued.** Shorter, lighter sessions while you readapt. This is what the ramp does.
  2. **Stretch the cramping muscle.** It's the standard acute treatment and fits the reflex model.
  3. **Something strongly flavoured (pickle juice).** A small study found it stopped induced cramps
     faster than water, too fast to be rehydration; the proposed mechanism is a reflex from the
     mouth and throat [22]. Interesting but thin evidence.
  4. **Hydration and electrolytes.** Reasonable if you're sweating a lot, but unlikely to be the
     main fix [19].

## What coaches do: the ramp

There's no single validated protocol, but common coaching practice lines up with the research:

1. **Cut volume first, intensity second.** Strength is intact for weeks, so loads can stay fairly
   close to normal; soreness is volume-driven, so sets come down more.
2. **Scale the starting point to the break:**

   | Break | Start load (of pre-break working weights / e1RM) | Start volume | Ramp |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 9–13 days | ~92% | ~60% | 2 sessions |
   | 2–4 weeks | ~88% | ~50% | 3 sessions |
   | 1–2 months | ~82% | ~50% | 4 sessions |
   | 2–4 months | ~78% | ~45% | ~6 sessions |
   | 4+ months | ~72% | ~40% | ~8 sessions, or run a dedicated comeback block |

3. **No max-effort sets early.** Drop AMRAPs and grinders for the first sessions; work at roughly
   RPE 6–7.
4. **Let the lifter beat the ramp.** If you hit your normal weights for the normal reps, you're back
   on that lift; there's no reason to keep it artificially light.
5. **Give the legs extra exposures.** Squat and other lower-body lifts get at least one more ramp
   exposure than upper-body lifts.
6. **Don't count ramp sessions as failures.** A missed rep in week one back says nothing about your
   real strength.

## Comeback programs

For a long break, a short dedicated block is often easier than ramping your regular program:

- **Return to Training (full body).** 2–4 weeks, 3 days a week, the big lifts every session at
  submaximal loads, volume climbing each week, then hand back to your regular program.
- **Squat Comeback.** Squat 3–4 times a week with 2–3 sets per session, starting around 70–80% of
  your pre-break e1RM and climbing a few percent a week. It starts with controlled tempo and
  progresses to paused and slow-eccentric work so soreness drops while exposure climbs.
